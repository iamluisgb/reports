[HOST_A] The papers. First, delay of gratification as a survival micro-benchmark for long-horizon agents — the Stanford marshmallow experiment rebuilt for ReAct agents running minute by minute with a question-raising tool under a per-step budget, with social context, personas and tool-use policy varied factorially. Nineteen thousand two hundred trajectories, Kaplan–Meier curves and discrete-time hazard models give time-resolved statistics on when agents give in.

[HOST_B] Honest work — and still only a micro-benchmark. Survival curves say when agents cave under social pressure, not why, and nineteen thousand runs of one scenario is one scenario. But the shape is right — evals that measure behaviour over time beat incident reports gathered after the fact.

[HOST_A] Second, a framework called M E T A for financial decisions — seven indicator agents, M A C D, R S I, A V W A P and Heikin-Ashi among them, feeding a decision agent with episodic memory. Past trades are stored as market-state embeddings with outcomes and reflections, retrieved at each decision. Third, Bayesian personalised value alignment — values as priors, context-dependent preferences as posteriors, corrected at inference time with no fine-tuning.

[HOST_B] Both argue the same thing from different directions — state matters. A trader that remembers its last trade and an alignment layer that re-estimates what you value per context both reject the static profile. The inference-time correction is the part to watch — no fine-tuning means cheap to deploy and cheap to remove.
