Papers — ArXiv
01
Vision-as-a-Tool: Visual Harness for Multimodal Agentic Coding
Agentic coding that needs to read screenshots, diagrams or rendered UIs is capped by the vision quality of whatever model hosts the agent — and text-only agents have none. Vision-as-a-Tool flips this: general-purpose multimodal models become callable external utilities, so a text-only agent offloads perception entirely rather than manipulating images for its own vision to inspect. The result is a harness where visual capability is a swappable dependency instead of an architectural constraint. [agents, tools, multimodal]
arXiv
02
OutfitterBench: Evaluating LLM Agents on Long-Horizon Resource Allocation and Commitment Coordination
Most agent benchmarks test single-episode competence; few test whether an agent's past commitments stay feasible as circumstances change. OutfitterBench runs agents an outdoor-equipment rental business for 200 days — a 46-product catalog, roughly 1,100 reservation requests, a fixed capital ceiling, and walk-in demand competing for reusable stock. Performance depends on keeping evolving purchase and reservation plans mutually consistent while pricing them against the alternatives each commitment forecloses. [benchmarks, planning, agents]
arXiv
03
Evaluating Large Language Models for Scientific Paper Search
Teams want local LLMs for literature retrieval to keep data in-house, but the hardware trade-offs are thinly documented. This paper measures inference latency from 512 to 128k input tokens on a Mac Studio versus a dual-A100 server, then evaluates eight open-weight models retrieving 70 papers from seven scientific repositories across three levels of query specificity. A100s are faster; unified memory lets the Mac load models the GPUs cannot. Retrieval success turns on model choice and query specificity — the local-vs-cloud question is per-workload, not per-faith. [retrieval, inference, local-models]
arXiv
04
Pixie: A Context-Aware Multi-Agent LLM Architecture for Digital Marketing
Marketing operations need agents that handle real-time and offline tasks against proprietary data and statistical models, not just public demos. Pixie pairs an orchestration agent with task-specific and reviewer agents, mixing zero-shot and few-shot prompting with expert strategy documents. Evaluated by trained marketing experts and output-variance measurement across single- and multi-agent workflows, the multi-agent configuration improves decision quality and consistency — a production deployment with assessment rather than a benchmark score. [multi-agent, orchestration, evaluation]

Why It Matters
▸ Two of today's papers and one launch converge on the same claim — capability now lives in the harness, not the weights. VaaT turns perception into a callable service, OpenCode's Browse gives agents a real browser instead of training-data guesses, and OutfitterBench judges agents on planning discipline rather than answer quality. If you are still benchmarking models in isolation, you are measuring the component that matters least; the competitive surface is tool plumbing, and it is the part you can actually control.
▸ The news is the shadow of the same shift. Windows fingerprinting installed agents, Optimizely's script tag weaponised, the 'lend' pattern for brokering scoped credentials — the agent stack has quietly become part of your supply chain and your telemetry surface without anyone signing off on it. Pixie shows multi-agent systems entering live enterprise workflows; the governance around them is being written by whoever ships first, as the Optimizely and telemetry stories demonstrate.
▸ OutfitterBench's premise — today's commitments detonate as tomorrow's constraints — is the through-line to Microsoft's July regression sitting latent for four months and the JPMorgan cascade taking other banks down with it. Long-horizon systems fail on feasibility drift, not on any single bad decision, which is exactly what staged rollouts and commitment-tracking benchmarks exist to catch. Agents managing real commitments for 200 days are a rehearsal for agents managing your infrastructure; treat the benchmark as a checklist for your own deployment.
▸ The local-inference paper's most useful finding is that the trade space is workload-shaped: A100s win on latency, unified memory wins on model size, and query specificity decides retrieval success more than either. The same reasoning applies to the harness decisions above — there is no default answer, only a fit between your constraints and the tooling you expose.
