01
OpenGray: a benchmark for evaluating the decisions of radiotherapy planning agents
Radiotherapy planning agents are usually scored on dose achieved, which says nothing about whether the decision to submit or escalate was correct. OpenGray adds tasks with provable answers to the OpenKBP-Opt head-and-neck dataset — including two dose-goal contradictions and a feasible control — and evaluates four systems from a rule-based script to a full LLM agent across 30 patients, plus 39 logged stereotactic radiosurgery runs of a clinical agent. Decision quality, problem recognition and technical failures are all recorded separately. [benchmark, clinical-ai, agent-evaluation]

02
Collective Emergent Alignment: Measuring and Amplifying Interaction-Induced Reasoning Alignment in Multi-Agent LLMs
Whether multi-agent LLM interaction produces alignment gains beyond what each agent achieves by self-refining alone is unmeasured territory. REMAP quantifies the effect via Semantic Coverage and interaction-specific alignment gains across model families and two multi-hop reasoning benchmarks. Interaction gains are broadly positive, concentrated on evidence distributed across agents, and track independently measured peer-information recovery — with composition-dependent heterogeneity and asymmetries. [multi-agent, emergent-behaviour, evaluation]
arXiv
03
SpectRAG: Weighted Chunk Graphs for Token-Budgeted Context Selection in Multi-Hop Retrieval-Augmented Generation
Dense retrieval ranks chunks independently, so multi-hop queries get redundant passages and missing cross-chunk dependencies. SpectRAG builds a weighted chunk graph — semantic similarity, document adjacency, section hierarchy, shared entities, citations — then runs spectral relevance propagation over it and selects context under an explicit token budget. The budgeted selection is the interesting part: most graph-RAG work stops at reranking and ignores the context window as a constraint. [rag, multi-hop, context-selection]
arXiv
04
Uncovering Why LLMs Fail: From Internal Mechanisms to Adversarial Reasoning Trajectories
Final-output correctness labels hide how a failure develops across a model's layers, and can't distinguish ordinary mistakes from adversarially induced ones. CauTrace is a white-box diagnostic framework that traces failures through seven defined functional states, representation propagation across transformer blocks, and temporal-state coordination of the reasoning trajectory. The claim under test: benign and adversarial failures occupy the same internal coordinates — if it holds, failure triage moves from output inspection to trajectory inspection. [interpretability, failure-analysis, diagnostics]

Why It Matters
▸ The day's real story is that every isolation assumption failed at once. Claude Code ships background-agent transcripts to the model, 'isolated' agents talk through the CPU cache, a fingerprinting script smuggles in something far worse, and macOS git still trusts compromised CAs months after the fact. Sandboxing at the process level, the package level and the certificate level all turned out to be weaker than advertised — and the agent era multiplies the blast radius, since the thing leaking is now your entire codebase context, not one file.
▸ Evaluation is migrating out of vendors' hands and into decision-level, licensed domains. Google shipped Gemini 4 Carbon with no published benchmarks and the community started running its own; OpenGray scores radiotherapy agents on whether the submit-or-escalate call was right, not on dose; Dayjob: Tax does the same for professional tax work. The pattern is consistent: aggregate benchmark scores are losing credibility, and the numbers that matter are per-decision, auditable, and drawn from work a licensed human would recognise. Expect procurement to follow.
▸ Coordination costs survive automation, and interaction cuts both ways. REMAP shows multi-agent interaction produces alignment gains self-refinement can't — but the cache side-channel story shows interaction also happens when you've explicitly forbidden it. Brooks's argument in the re-read piece holds: adding agents is adding manpower, with the same communication overhead and a new channel for it to leak through. The residual-knowledge essay lands the point — what you must still know is precisely what you can no longer verify by reading the code, because nobody wrote it.
